## Naming and conceptual audit

The old heading **Regression Models and GEE** presented generalized estimating equations as though they were an umbrella for all correlated regression. The published literature does not support that interpretation. GEE is a class of estimating-equation approaches particularly useful for **marginal mean models** with correlated longitudinal or clustered outcomes, generally without a complete joint law for outcomes within a cluster. Its standard variance arguments rely on a specified estimand, suitable mean/variance assumptions and an asymptotic regime of independent clusters. Correlation of observations *within* a cluster does not replace independence *between* clusters; ordinary sandwich standard errors can be inaccurate when the number of independent clusters is small.

GLMMs and hierarchical regressions instead specify random/latent effects and conditional response models, with likelihood-based or Bayesian inference. Their regression parameters can be cluster/subject-specific rather than population-averaged; a nonlinear-link marginal coefficient is not generally equal to its conditional counterpart. GAMLSS extends regression to response-distribution parameters beyond the mean, and GAM smooths covariate-response relationships; neither is a synonym for GEE, and the ordinary GAMLSS formulation assumes conditional independence given specified predictors, parameters and random effects. The selected user-facing title is therefore **Regression Models and Estimating Equations / Modelos de Regressão e Equações de Estimação**: broader than GEE without implying that the named techniques are the only estimation methods.

## Three concise bilingual research routes

1. **Generalized Linear Models and GEE / Modelos lineares generalizados e GEE.** Begin with GLM support, links and variance; continue to Liang–Zeger marginal models, working correlation, sandwich/Godambe uncertainty and comparisons with subject-specific effects. Students should be able to articulate why a wrong working correlation does not necessarily bias a correctly specified marginal mean estimator under suitable regularity, yet mean misspecification, few independent clusters or unaddressed informative missingness may invalidate naive inference. Foundational original: [Liang & Zeger (1986)](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/73.1.13), *Biometrika* **73**(1), 13–22. Conceptual comparison: [Zeger, Liang & Albert (1988)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2531734), *Biometrics* **44**(4), 1049–1060. Practical specialist introduction: Hardin & Hilbe, second edition.
2. **Mixed-Effects and Hierarchical Regression / Regressão com efeitos mistos e modelos hierárquicos.** Introduce conditional regression, observation and latent-effect layers, parameter/hyperparameter uncertainty and frequentist versus Bayesian fitting. Original GLMM reference: [Breslow & Clayton (1993)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1993.10594284), *JASA* **88**(421), 9–25. Applied spatial Bayesian bridge: Moraga's reproducible 2019 geospatial-health text, with latent Gaussian models, INLA and SPDE. INLA and SPDE are model/approximation-specific, not arbitrary GEE estimators or universal solvers for unpublished conditional spatial ARMA models.
3. **Flexible and Distributional Regression / Regressão flexível e distribucional.** Begin with Wood's GAM smooth/penalized-spline treatment; then study Rigby–Stasinopoulos's GAMLSS for response distributions with location, scale and shape parameters linked to covariates, including skewed, heavy-tailed or positive data. This bridges to SAR measurements and environmental/medical imaging; modelling spatial or temporal correlation still requires an additional coherent dependence mechanism and validated inference.

Cross-guide links are limited to `#spatial-models` (spatial dependence/latent fields), `#theory` (estimating functions and asymptotics), `#sar` (non-Gaussian image responses) and `#causal` (a regression association or GEE coefficient does **not** itself identify a causal effect). Moraga's book is a **published methodological reference**, not a claim of the site owner's medical or Bayesian spatial publications.
